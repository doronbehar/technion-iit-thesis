\chapter{Cooling Simulation Results}\label{chap:results}

Before trapping both \ce{Yb^+} and \ce{CHDBrI^+}, we wanted to make sure we are able to cool efficiently only \ce{Yb^+}. We believe that cooling fast the \ce{Yb^+} ions, must help cool fast the \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions. This led us to investigate first how to cool \ce{Yb^+} alone fast, and the main results of this research are in section~\ref{sec:results/laserAngles}. Next we added \ce{CHDBrI^+} and varied the intensities and the initial temperatures, to see how sensitive is the sympathetic cooling to these parameters.

\section{Laser Cooling Intrusion Angle(s) \& Trap Geometry}\label{sec:results/laserAngles}

When our experimentalists first tried to cool the \ce{Yb^+}, they had less then a total of $1\mathrm{mW}$ of power. This summons testing: Is using 1 laser enough? If so, which intrusion angle to use it with? If we split the all our power to multiple laser beams, which angles to use?

In the case of 1 laser beam, the naive choice might be to use the $z$ axis, which is the symmetry axis of the trap, and in which there is also no micromotion. It turns out that using the $z$ axis is actually the worst choice of them all, and in fact you \emph{do} want the laser beam to mix the principal axes of the trap as much as possible. The next sections describe how we parameterized this, and why we think mixing the principal axes helps.

No matter how many lasers we use, we give them \textbf{all of them} a single intensity $I \propto \Omega/\Gamma$. This means that comparing 3 lasers of a total intensity of $I$, to a single laser of the same intensity, would require using $\Omega \propto \sqrt{I/3}$ in the 3 lasers simulations. For a total intensity of $\Omega/\Gamma = 1$ dividing into $3$ laser beams, we conclude each of them should be using $\Omega/\Gamma \approx 0.57$.

In the results presented below, markers represent the intensities of \textbf{each one} of the lasers, and colors represent laser angles \emph{group}s. A group includes one or more lasers. They were performed with a laser detuning of $\delta/\Gamma = -2.6$, over $1000$ \ce{Yb^+} ions and in a trap with frequencies $(f_x, f_y, f_z, f_\mathrm{rf}) = (8.5, 8.5, 1.5, 100)\ \mathrm{kHz}$.

\subsection{Simulation Results}

In figure~\ref{fig:laserAngles-temperatures}, we see that with a total intensity of $\Omega/\Gamma = 1$, we can get a wide range of cooling rates. The cooling rates are summarized in table~\ref{tbl:laserAngles-cooling-rates}. The propensity rule we derive from this comparison is that mixing the principal axes of the trap is what helps cool faster.

This is not only a matter of breaking the cylindrical symmetry of the trap, as e.g the $x$ laser does that too, and it cools slower then $r^-$, which is almost parallel to it. We also think there is no meaning to the $\phi$ direction of all laser angles, and the most important parameter is how much the laser is mixing $z$ principal axis movement with the $x,y$ plane.

If obliged to use one laser, using $e^-$ gives slightly better results in comparison to $r^-$, but the latter is parallel to the optical table, and hence is slightly easier to setup technically in the lab. This is why we chose $r^-$ for the next scan, in which we also added \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions and modulated the intensity ($\Omega$), in multiple initial temperatures.

\subsection{\ce{Yb^+} Temperatures in the Presence of \ce{CHDBrI^+}}

In figure~\ref{fig:Yb-cooling-limit:T_i10:T} we can see the temperatures of only the \ce{Yb^+} ions, with colors for different intensities. Intriguingly, we see they stabilize to a certain value, after an intensity dependent amount of time. When we looked at the animations for each of those lines, we saw a varying number of rogue \ce{Yb^+} outside of the crystal, that are precluded from joining it, due to \ce{CHDBrI^+} that have already adsorbed. In figure~\ref{fig:Yb-cooling-limit:T_i10:p:O1} we see an example of this phenomenon, with roughly 10 rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions.

In the examples above both species started at $10\ \mathrm{K}$, but if we start at $5\ \mathrm{K}$, we \emph{are} capable of assembling a fuller \ce{Yb^+} crystal without rogue ions. This is apparent in the animation snapshot in figure~\ref{fig:Yb-cooling-limit:T_i5:p:O2.5}, where we also used an intensity of $\Omega / \Gamma = 2.5$. \subsection{Sympathetic Cooling with Varying Intensities}

In figure~\ref{fig:sympathetic-omega-dep:T:m222} we can see the \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions' temperatures for initial temperatures $\{5, 10\}\mathrm{K}$, and with a different color per laser intensity. It seems that in this range of intensities (targeting only \ce{Yb^+} ions of course), one can conclude that a stronger laser helps cooling. This is easily justified by expression~\ref{eq:laser-cool-force} of the laser cooling force introduced in section~\ref{sec:theory/cooling}, and also by the phenomenon of power broadening\cite{SteckCooling}.

\begin{figure}

The existence of rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions, might suggest that for cooling the \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions efficiently, we might want to cool the \ce{Yb^+} ions not as fast as possible - with say $\Omega = 0.4\Gamma$. This indeed might increase the total number of crystallized \ce{CHDBrI^+} after a long time. However we focus in this research on improving the cooling on time scales of roughly $50\ \mathrm{ms}$, so we decided to stick with the attempt to cool the \ce{Yb^+} ions as fast as we can.

A more intriguing phenomenon we noticed in the $\Omega/\Gamma \geq 1$ lines of figure~\ref{fig:sympathetic-omega-dep:T:m222}, is the inconsistent cooling rates (slopes). More explicitly, the descent is steeper when you start colder, and seems to decay independently of temperature you start at. To illustrate this phenomena more clearly, we took the $\Omega/\Gamma = 1$ lines, and continued the $T_i = 10\ \mathrm{K}$ simulation for $20$ more $\mathrm{ms}$. In figure~\ref{fig:sympathetic-Ti-dep4m222}, we show both lines while giving the $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ line a simple time offset of $37.0\ \mathrm{ms}$, such that the lines meet when $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ starts cooling.

We see in figure~\ref{fig:sympathetic-omega-dep:T:m222} that when the \ce{CHDBrI^+}'s temperature starts at $T_i = 10\ \mathrm{K}$, it descends slower when it reaches $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ - slower then if it had started at $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ initially! We identify this phenomenon as \emph{hysteresis} - the \ce{CHDBrI^+}'s cooling rate seem to depend on the initial temperature on long time scales, and that limits their cooling rate.

Our attempts to fit our results to the models presented in section~\ref{sec:theory/models} failed due to the hysteresis. We managed to identify the root cause for the conservation of energy, when we looked at the animations, as described in the next section.

\section{Angular Momentum Conservation}

As depicted in figure~\ref{fig:angular-momentum-conserved}, we noticed that many gas-phase \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions are rotating around the crystal. Their rotation axis is the $z$ axis, which is also the symmetry axis of our cylindrically symmetric trap, meaning $f_x = f_y > f_z$.

This collective movement around the $z$ axis limits the cooling rate because the ions don't have any reason to collide with the ion crystal, and thereby lose kinetic energy. This is the core of our disagreement with Baba \& Waki's model described in section~\ref{sec:theory/models} - the angular momentum, and no other vector like variables are treated in their model.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{graphics/CHDBrI-Rotating-around-z.pdf}
	\caption{A snapshot of an animation in which one can clearly see the ions are rotating around the $z$ axis - which is also the symmetry axis of the cylindrically shaped trap.}
	\label{fig:angular-momentum-conserved}

Since the sympathetic cooling rate naturally and evidently depends strongly on the collision rates, we tried to increase the secular frequencies, to increase the collision rates. We scanned multiple sets of trap frequencies, all with a cylindrically symmetry, and the results are described in the next section.

\section{Varying Secular Frequencies}

In this scan of many different trap frequencies, we wanted to eliminate the effects of rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions we saw on  section~\ref{sec:results/sympathetic-omega-dep}. We did that by ensuring they are all cooled almost instantly, using multiple high intensity lasers. We also chose secular frequencies with similar ratios between $f_x = f_y$ and $f_z$ - to reduce the risk to mix effects related to the trap's eccentricity. Lastly, these simulation also were with $299$ \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions and $701$ \ce{Yb^+} ions, and the parameters presented in table~\ref{tbl:trap_dependence_strong_laser}.

In all of these simulations we had $4$ different initial conditions per trap and initial temperature. We found consistently rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions only for $f_r = (8.5,8.5,1.5)$ and $f_r = (17,17,3)$. Their amounts for for these traps, with $T_i = 10\ \mathrm{K}$ was (respectively) $7.25\pm 2.3$ and $5.5\pm 1$. The amount of rogue \ce{Yb^+} for $T_i = 20\ \mathrm{K}$, was roughly twice as much.

Never the less, the lower amounts of rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions is observable in their temperatures, as depicted in figure~\ref{fig:crystal-stronger-trap:Ti20}. The absence of rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions in the traps given there, allows us to observe fast and efficient laser cooling in the presence of a sympathetically cooled ions, with rates of almost $1 \mathrm{kHz}$\footnote{Calculated with PieceWise Linear Fits\cite{pwlf}.}.

The next thing we show, is how much better is the sympathetic cooling with the stronger traps. In figure~\ref{fig:trap-comparison:T:m222} we see a similar $T(t)$ plot with various colors and markers per traps and initial temperatures, respectively. Clearly using a stronger trap almost always helps, especially with high initial temperatures. This rule of thumb is intuitive since higher secular frequencies induce more collisions between \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions and the ion crystal.

However, with $T_i = 10\ \mathrm{K}$, and comparing $f_r = (17,17,3)$ v.s $f_r = (15, 15, 2.5)$, we observe a violation of this rule of thumb. Our best explanation for this violation is the $5.5\pm 1$ rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions we counted earlier for $f_r = (17,17,3)$, which seem to undermine the sympathetic cooling.

When comparing $f_r = (5.5,5.5,1)$ v.s $f_r = (8.5,8.5,1.5)$, we don't see such a violation, even though we for $f_r = (8.5,8.5,1.5)$ even more rogue ions - $7.25\pm 2.3$. We think that the higher secular frequencies inducing more \ce{CHDBrI^+} collisions compete with the disturbance of the rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions.
