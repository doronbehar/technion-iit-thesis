\section{LASER Cooling Intrusion Angle(s) \& Trap Geometry}\label{sec:results/laserAngles}

To date, in our lab, the amount of LASER power we have available for LASER cooling our \ce{Yb^+} is scarce. If obliged to use a single LASER angle, the intuitive choice might be to use the $z$ axis, which is the symmetry axis of the trap, and in which there is also no micromotion. Apparently, the main conclusion from this endeavor is that you \emph{want} to break the symmetry of the trap, and using the $z$ axis is actually the worst choice of them all. The next paragraphs describe how we modeled this, and why we think breaking the symmetry helps.

Our vacuum chamber has only a finite set of windows we can use, so we gave each of them a name, as depicted in figure~\ref{fig:vacuum-chamber-laserAngles}. Each intrusion window is mapped to a $(\theta, \phi)$ pair, as listed in table~\ref{tbl:laserAngles-list}, according to the standard spherical solid angle parametrization depicted in figure~\ref{fig:laserAngles-solid-angle}.

After observing these results we realized we might not be able to completely crystallize all \ce{Yb^+} ions with only a single LASER. However, this may not be a devastating issue for cooling the \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions, as it might still be possible to cool it efficiently.

We conclude from these results that generally speaking, cooling the \ce{Yb^+} ions faster is better also for cooling the \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions. Although we often see rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions outside the crystal, one might argue that for cooling the \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions efficiently, we might want to cool the \ce{Yb^+} ions not as fast as possible - with say $\Omega = 0.4\Gamma$. This indeed might increase the total number of crystallized \ce{CHDBrI^+} after a long time. However we focus in this research on improving the cooling on time scales of roughly $50\ \mathrm{ms}$.

A more intriguing phenomena we noticed in the $\Omega/\Gamma \geq 1$ lines of figure~\ref{fig:sympathetic-omega-dep:T:m222}, is the inconsistent cooling rates (slopes) of the lines. More precisely, the descend is stronger when you start colder, and seems to decay no matter what temperature you start at. To illustrate this phenomena more clearly, we took the $\Omega/\Gamma = 1$ lines, and continued the $T_i = 10\ \mathrm{K}$ simulation for $20$ more $\mathrm{ms}$. We then plotted both lines while giving the $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ line a simple time offset of $37.0\ \mathrm{ms}$ - such that the lines meet when $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ starts cooling. We see in figure~\ref{fig:sympathetic-omega-dep:T:m222} that when the \ce{CHDBrI^+}'s temperature starts at $T_i = 10\ \mathrm{K}$, it descends slower when it reaches $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ - slower then if it had started at $T_i = 5\ \mathrm{K}$ initially! We identify this phenomenon as \emph{hysteresis} - the \ce{CHDBrI^+}'s cooling rate seem to depend on the initial temperature on long time scales, and that limits their cooling rate. 

Before we were sure we observe a hysteresis, we tried to find models in literature and even to model ourselves the sympathetic cooling rate, and this venture is described in the next section.

\section{Modelling Attempts}

Most models we found\cite{Plasma2T1,Plasma2T2,BabaWakiSympatheticCooling} did not include explicitly all parameters we can tweak here such as the LASER intensity, detuning. Not only that, the authors of these models were usually concerned with the energy exchange rate between the different species, as a function of each species' temperatures. As we were hoping in this research venture to establish propensity rules for effective cooling, the first drawbacks of these models was their analytical complexity.

Even if one accepts the analytical complexity, the main problem we found with these models, is that they did not describe any such behavior as this hysteresis. To establish this argument, let's see a main result of Baba \& Waki's model\cite{BabaWakiSympatheticCooling}, which is also not incomprehensibly complex, and is depicted in figure~\ref{fig:babaWakiModel}.

Their model is essentially a differential equation, that involves $\partial_t T_\mathrm{sc}$, $T_\mathrm{sc}(t)$ and $T_\mathrm{lc}(t)$. Other parameters included there are ion trap geometrical parameters, and the ions' masses. Any form of a differential equation that treats only these scalar thermodynamic variables, is likely to fail to recognise and predict any form of hysteresis.

After unsuccessful attempts to fit a model to our results, we did manage to explain why the temperatures stop descending after a certain point, by looking at the animations. As depicted in figure~\ref{fig:angular-momentum-conserved}, we noticed when looking closely, that many gas-phase \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions are rotating around the crystal. The rotating axis is the $z$ axis, which is also the symmetry axis of our cylindrically symmetric trap, meaning $f_x = f_y > f_z$.

This collective movement around the $z$ axis limits the cooling rate because the ions don't have any reason to collide with the ion crystal, and thereby lose kinetic energy. It is only natural for the angular momentum of the \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions to be conserved in such a system, as no force induced on them can break the symmetry.

Although we encountered this limitation, we still tried to improve our cooling rates while still using cylindrically symmetric traps, but of different frequencies. This is described in the next section.

\section{Varying Secular Frequencies}

In this scan of many different trap frequencies, we wanted to eliminate the effects of rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions we saw on  section~\ref{sec:results/sympathetic-omega-dep}. We did that by ensuring they are all cooled almost instantly, using multiple high intensity LASERs. We also chose per trap, secular frequencies with similar ratios between $f_x = f_y$ and $f_z$ - to reduce the risk to mix effects related to the crystal eccentricity. Lastly, these simulation also were with $299$ \ce{CHDBrI^+} ions and $701$ \ce{Yb^+} ions, and the shared parameters of them all are available in table~\ref{tbl:trap_dependence_strong_laser}.

The next thing we checked was how much better is the sympathetic cooling with the stronger traps. In figure~\ref{fig:trap-comparison:T:m222} we see a similar $T(t)$ plot with various colors and markers per traps and initial temperatures, respectively. Clearly using a stronger trap almost always helps, especially with high initial temperatures.

This propensity rule however is violated when there are rogue \ce{Yb^+} ions. In the results presented in figure~\ref{fig:trap-comparison:T:m222}, when beginning at $T_i = 10\ \mathrm{K}$, the $f_r = (15, 15, 2.5)$ line reaches lower temperatures then $f_r = (17,17,3)$, and hence disagrees with this propensity rule. When looking at the animations of $f_r = (17,17,3)$, we observe roughly 5 \ce{Yb^+} ions out of the crystal - similarly to figure~\ref{fig:Yb-cooling-limit:T_i10:p:O1}. These \ce{Yb^+} undermine the sympathetic cooling.
