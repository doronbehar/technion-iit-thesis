\subsection{What are Chiral Molecules?}\label{sec:intro/about-chirality}

The word chirality is derived from the Greek word for 'hand'. We define an object as chiral if it is distinguishable from its mirror image, with the classic example of our hands. Alternatively, the two mirror images of a chiral object, usually referred to as the left and right hands of the object, cannot be superimposed (see figure~\ref{fig:chirality-with-hands}). This also means they cannot be related by rotational and/or translational transformations alone. The first documented observation of chirality in molecules was of L. Pasteur in 1848, where he examined salt crystals and found they appear in two mirrored structures\cite{PasteurChirality}.

\begin{figure}
	\centering\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{graphics/chirality_with_hands.pdf}
	\caption{The two hands of a chiral molecule, also known as enantiomers, cannot be superimposed. In other words, mapping the enantiomers to each other can't be done by rotations and/or translations alone, but requires a mirror transformation.}\label{fig:chirality-with-hands}
\end{figure}

Chirality is a fundamental property of many molecules in nature, referring to their existence in two mirror-image forms called \emph{enantiomers}. A striking demonstration of chirality can be found in the molecule \emph{limonene}, which can be found in citrus fruits and silver fir (Abies alba). The two enantiomers of limonene have identical molecular formulas and with mirrored structure, yet they interact differently with olfactory receptors in the human nose\cite{LimoneneSmell}. This difference in perception, caused solely by molecular chirality, demonstrates how profoundly chirality can influence biological interactions.

Despite the structural symmetry allowed by chemistry, nature consistently uses only one enantiomer in living organisms in many biologically relevant molecules. This phenomenon is known as \emph{homochirality}, and it is not mandated by fundamental physics, yet it is universally observed across all known forms of life. Figure~\ref{fig:homo-chirality} illustrates several such examples.

\subsection{Ion Trapping}\label{sec:intro/ion-trap}

The basic challenge of trapping charged particles is to overcome the limitation of the Laplace equation:

\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\phi = 0
\label{eq:laplace-original}
\end{equation}

Meaning that we cannot get a point in space in which the potential is at a minima in all spatial dimensions. As depicted in figure~\ref{fig:ion-trap-basics}, we can artificially overcome this limitation by imposing a force resembling a saddle-point, and oscillate the sign of it. If the oscillation if fast enough, a mass in the center will not have enough time to escape the origin in the direction of the maxima, before it will become a minima. In the context of ion trapping, the oscillation frequency is usually in the RF regime, and is denoted $\omega_\mathrm{rf} \equiv 2\pi f_\mathrm{rf}$.

\section{Simulations Overview}\label{sec:intro/simulation}

Since the main mechanism of sympathetic cooling is Coulomb interactions, we chose to simulate trapping and cooling with LAMMPS\cite{LAMMPS}. It is a time propagation software package, capable of utilizing the GPU for simulating numerous classical models relevant in many contexts. In our case, we selected a simple model of charged point particles with only long-range Coulomb interactions.

Given $N$ particles and $C$ available GPU cores, the amount of Coulomb interactions LAMMPS is capable of computing all at once is $N(N-1)/(2C)$. With 1000 ions, and our GPU, we found that the bottleneck of such simulations is writing the results to disk, and not the computing the Coulomb interactions\footnote{More technical details on how we save results to disk throughout a simulation are available in chapter~\ref{chap:software}.\yotam{move to the main text, it is not a footnote}}.
