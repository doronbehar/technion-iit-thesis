\section{Motivation}

\subsection{What are Chiral Molecules?}\label{sec:intro/about-chirality}

% TODO: this is copied too verbatim from Itay Erez's thesis. Use ChatGPT to rephrase these 2 paragraphs.

The word chirality is derived from the Greek word for ”hand”. We define an object as chiral if it is distinguishable from its mirror image, with the classic example of our hands. In other words, the two mirror images of a chiral object, usually referred to as the left and right hands of the object, cannot be superimposed. That means they cannot be related by rotational and/or translational transformations alone. The first documented observation of chirality was of L. Pasteur in 1848, where he examined salt crystals and found they appear in two mirrored structures\cite{PasteurChirality}.

A molecule consists of multiple atoms bound together. Molecules of 2 or 3 atoms must be linear or planar, therefore they must exhibit an internal reflection symmetry about, at least, one of their planes. On the other hand, a molecule of 4 or more atoms, can take a 3D form which might exhibit no reflection symmetries at all. In this case, the molecule is chiral since its two mirror images, i.e. left and right hands, are distinguishable, as the example in figure~\ref{fig:chirality-with-hands}. The two hands of a chiral molecule are often called enantiomers.

\begin{figure}
	\centering\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{graphics/chirality_with_hands.pdf}
	\caption{The two hands of a chiral molecule, also known as enantiomers, cannot be superimposed. In other words, mapping the enantiomers to each other can’t be done by rotations and/or translations alone, but requires a mirror transformation.}\label{fig:chirality-with-hands}
\end{figure}

Apparently, opposite enantiomers of the same molecule can have substantially different chemical properties, although having an almost identical structure. A classic example is that of glucose \& fructose - two enantiomers of sugars found mostly in white sugar and fruits, respectively. Apparently, when you shine light over liquid solutions containing these types of sugar, they will each rotate the light polarization differently, allowing you to easily measure the concentration of each enantiomer\cite{sugarChirality}. This specific measurement scheme of chirality works for sugars for non-obvious reasons, and cannot be generalized to any pair of chiral molecules.

The existence of glucose \& fructose mostly in white sugar \& fruits respectively, is in fact an example of a broader natural phenomenon called homo-chirality - meaning nature's preference of 1 chirality in certain contexts, over another. Sugar, and other examples for homo-chirality in nature are depicted in figure~\ref{fig:homo-chirality}.

In contrast to other chemically analytical methods, we wish to study the effect of chirality over the vibrational spectrum of chiral molecules. In the standard model, the only elementary particles that interact differently with other elementary left and right particles, are the $Z$ and $W$ bosons. This symmetry braking mechanism is called parity violation, and therefor the energy difference between the opposite enantiomers' vibrations is termed $\Delta_\mathrm{PV}$.

\subsection{Why Chiral Molecular Ions?}\label{sec:intro/why-molecular-ions}

In most chiral molecules, $\Delta_\mathrm{PV} \ll 1 \mathrm{Hz}$, where the transitions are in the mid \& far IR regime. However, a study of the differences in the vibrational spectra of opposite enantiomers of several chiral molecules, has shown that \ce{CHDBrI^+} has a maximal $\Delta_\mathrm{PV} \approx 1.8 \mathrm{Hz}$\cite{EduardusCHDBrI}, as depicted in figure~\ref{fig:EduardusCHDBrI}.

The existence of multiple vibrational transitions in a molecule, and trying to measure a specific vibration of a high energy, can cause a phenomenon termed Intra Vibrational Redistribution (IVR). The term describes the potential of the energy of an excited high-energy vibration, to be redistributed into a combination of multiple lower energy vibrations. To assess the possibility of this to happen, I wrote a small Python script that computes this. The computation method and the results are given in chapter~\ref{chap:IVR}, and were reported briefly in out published research too\cite{IVR_article}.

\subsection{Why Cool the Molecular Ions Fast?}\label{sec:intro/why-cool-fast}

To measure $\Delta_\mathrm{PV}$, we decided use Ramsey Ramsey spectroscopy. The complete discussion that led to this decision\cite{IVR_article,EduardusCHDBrI,ItayErezThesis} is out of scope for this thesis, but here I'll list 2 advantages of Ramsey spectroscopy that have particularly motivated this research: (1) We don't need to tune ultra-narrow CW-IR LASERs, and instead we only need to tune the time duration between pulses; (2) We can directly map decoherence effects in the time domain, and not the frequency domain.

Performing Ramsey spectroscopy on molecular ions, has a few more implications further motivating this research: (1) Molecular Ions are trappable for long times and therefor can be cooled and be coherent; (2) We can't use fluorescence for state detection due to too low ion number, but we can use photo-dissociation, as depicted in figure~\ref{fig:photo-dissociation}.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{graphics/Photo-Dissociation.pdf}
	\caption{Illustration of a molecule's dissociation, done with a photon of energy $\hbar \omega_{PD}$. If the photon's energy was tuned right, the molecule will be dissociated, depending on the energy level it was in.}
	\label{fig:photo-dissociation}
\end{figure}

The important implication of using photo-dissociation state detection after a Ramsey spectroscopy sequence, is that we need to reload (synthesize) new molecules for every Ramsey sequence. Not only that, we must remember that in Ramsey spectroscopy, the uncertainty in a frequency measurement goes like $1/\left(\tau \sqrt{N}\right)$, were $\tau$ is the coherence time. Increasing $\tau$ (by cooling) will increases the amount of periods after which we focus the measurement efforts, and thus decreases the uncertainty even further, as depicted in figure~\ref{fig:ramsey-uncertainty}.

\begin{figure}

	\label{fig:ramsey-uncertainty}
\end{figure}

\subsection{How to Cool a Molecular Ion?}

Cooling a molecule or a molecular ion is usually very hard. To get temperatures $<1\mathrm{K}$ can only be done with LASER cooling which requires a cycling transition that can be modeled as a 2 level system. Complex enough molecules such as ours, have too many degrees of freedom, that makes it very hard to find a cycling transition as you typically find in LASER cooled atoms.

The solution we went with is to use sympathetic cooling - a process in which 2 species of atoms are trapped together, one of them is directly LASER cooled, and via kinetic collisions the LASER cooled atom sucks up the kinetic energy of the other atom too. In our case we are talking about ions, and we chose to LASER cool an ion too, meaning the collisions are coulomb collisions, and the energy exchange is frequent and efficient.\footnote{For a comparative sympathetic cooling method, see Hudson's review of sympathetic cooling of molecular ions using LASER cooled \emph{neutral} atoms\cite{HudsonSympatheticCooling}.}

To ensure efficient kinetic energy exchange between the LASER cooled (lc) and sympathetically cooled (sc) ions, we chose to use \ce{Yb^+} ions as LASER cooled ions, because their mass ($m_\mathrm{lc} = 174\mathrm{amu}$) is not too far from \ce{CHDBrI^+}'s mass ($m_\mathrm{sc} = 222\mathrm{amu}$), and they are known to have a LASER cooling transition\cite{RansfordThesis}. This choice is both intuitive, and is somewhat justified by Baba \& Waki's numerical study on the subject\cite{BabaWakiSympatheticCooling}, in the context of linear Paul traps (a bit different kind of ion traps).

This decision dictates the two main experimental components this thesis focuses upon - trapping \& cooling, both of which optimized for fast cooling. The models of both trapping \& cooling are discussed in detail in chapter~\ref{chap:theory}.

\subsection{Ion Trapping}\label{sec:intro/ion-trap}

The basic challenge of trapping charged particles is to overcome the limitation of the Laplace equation:

\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\phi = 0
\label{eq:laplace-original}
\end{equation}

Meaning that we cannot get a point in space in which the potential is at a minima in all spatial dimensions. As depicted in figure~\ref{fig:ion-trap-basics}, we can artificially overcome this limitation by imposing a force resembling a saddle-point, and oscillate the sign of it such that a mass in the center doesn't have time to escape much the center, before it experiencing a centering force in that direction. The saddle-point oscillation RF frequencies, is denoted in this research with $\omega_\mathrm{rf} \equiv 2\pi f_\mathrm{rf}$.

On time scales much longer then the RF period, an ion experiencing such an oscillating potential, \emph{is} experiencing a force of lower frequency frequency denoted $\omega_\mathrm{sec} = 2\pi f_\mathrm{sec}$, as depicted in figure~\ref{fig:micromotion-illustrated}.

Worth noting here though, is the fact that typical ion traps induce secular frequencies of $\sim 100\ \mathrm{kHz}$. Our ion trap however, is unique in the sense it is designed for much lower secular frequencies of usually less then $10\ \mathrm{kHz}$. Also our typical RF frequencies are lower then typical - $\sim 100\ \mathrm{kHz}$ v.s frequencies of at least a few $\mathrm{MHz}$.

We haven't found any evidence even for simple LASER cooling in traps of such secular frequencies, so we wanted to verify this is possible first in simulations. Additionally, our ion trap is connected to an FPGA and home-built electrical drivers\cite{ElianaThesis}, allowing us to trap our ions in a range of secular frequencies, inviting us to study the effect of different secular frequencies over the cooling efficiency.

\subsection{Our Velocity Map Imaging}\label{sec:intro/VMI}

One last motivating point for cooling the ions fast, is our detection scheme based upon a Velocity Map Imaging (VMI) system optimized\cite{ElianaThesis} for our research. The basic idea is to shutdown the ion trap's electric fields after the photo-dissociation is done, and then focus the ions to a detector of charged particles with spatial resolution. The key feature is that different kinetic energies are mapped to different points in the detector, allowing you to infer the kinetic energy distribution of the particles you had before releasing them. This mapping has limited resolution and was optimized as much as possible\cite{ElianaThesis}.

\section{Simulations Overview}\label{sec:intro/simulation}

Since the main mechanism of sympathetic cooling is Coulomb interactions, we chose to simulate trapping and cooling with LAMMPS\cite{LAMMPS}. It is a time propagation software package, capable of calculating $N(N-1)$ coulomb interactions very fast using the GPU - faster then writing the results to disk. LAMMPS is capable of simulating numerous classical models relevant in many contexts, but in our case, we picked a simple model of charged point particles with only long-range coulomb interactions.

\subsection{Initial Conditions}

To gather conclusions from the simulations as accurately as possible, we wanted to initiate the simulation in a state that is thermodynamically stable as much as possible, to avoid two undesired phenomena: (1) 'Sloshing', which is the collective movement of the ion cloud in high amplitudes over the origin, and (2) 'breathing', which is a collective movement of the ions inwards and outwards periodically. The challenge of choosing initial conditions is discussed in detail in chapter~\ref{chap:theory}.

\subsection{Temperatures Measurements}

Another detail taken care of thoroughly in this work, is the temperature measurements. Apparently, given a 2D array of a velocity distribution of $N$ ions in space, there are $2^5 - 1$ different ways to compute the temperature. These are described in detail in chapter~\ref{chap:theory} too.

\subsection{Convergence}\label{sec:theory/convergence}

Just like in every simulation based research, one has to make sure their results converge, and that is mainly dictated by the time step. Convergence mean that no matter if you lower the time steps, the results stay pretty much the same. LAMMPS is particularly comfortable for controlling the time step, and tuning it such that simulations don't take too long, but are accurate enough. In chapter~\ref{chap:theory} we also discuss how we picked the time step of the simulation with respect to trapping model in particular, and we prove that our time step choice is good enough to ensure convergence for most cases.
